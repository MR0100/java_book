# Mock: Compensation Negotiation (Verbatim)

This is a verbatim-style transcript of a **compensation negotiation** — the conversation that happens *after* you have already passed the loop and the company wants to hire you. It is not an interview; the technical evaluation is over and you have leverage you never had before. The transcript runs about thirty minutes of actual talking, spread across two phone calls a few days apart: the verbal-offer call where the recruiter floats a number, and the follow-up call where the revised package is agreed and confirmed. The two people are a recruiter (call him Marcus) whose job is to close you efficiently and within budget, and a candidate (call her Priya) who has done her homework and has a real alternative in hand.

Read it the way you'd run your own negotiation. **The single most important reframe: this is collaborative, not adversarial.** Marcus is not your enemy — he is a person whose incentive is to *get you to yes* without blowing his budget or his level guidance. The goal of the whole conversation is a mutual yes: a number you'll happily accept and a package his comp team will approve. Everything below — the deflections, the anchor, the "this is our best" pushback — is in service of finding that overlap, while staying warm enough that Marcus stays on your side. At each turn, cover the coaching callout and predict the right move first, then read the callout to check yourself. The candidate here is *strong-but-human*: at one point she nearly blurts her current salary, which would have anchored the entire negotiation against her, and a callout flags the near-miss before she recovers. This is a representative mock; the numbers are fictional round figures chosen to make the mechanics legible.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Where You'll See This / Why This Matters.** This is the single highest-paid half-hour of your career, and almost no one practices it. Think about the leverage at this exact moment: the company has spent weeks of engineer-time interviewing you, the hiring manager has emotionally committed to you, the panel has written you up, the requisition is approved, and the recruiter is now *measured on closing you*. They want a yes more than you want the job. And yet most candidates, exhausted and relieved after the loop, accept the first number within minutes — leaving a stack of money on the table that compounds for years.
>
> Run the arithmetic on why this conversation is worth taking seriously. Suppose this thirty-minute call moves your year-one total from the opening **₹54L** to the final **₹92L** — a **₹38L** difference. That is not a one-time bump. Every future raise is computed as a *percentage* of a base that is now higher; every equity refresh is anchored to a band you entered higher in; your *next* job's offer will be benchmarked against this comp. Carry even a fraction of that delta forward over a 25-year career, with raises and refreshes stacking on top, and a single well-run negotiation is worth a **large lifetime sum** — frequently more than a year's salary, sometimes several. There is almost nothing else you can do in thirty minutes that pays like this.
>
> And the cost of trying is essentially zero. **Companies do not rescind offers because a candidate negotiated politely and professionally.** They expect it; the first number usually has room built in precisely because they expect you to ask. The real risk is not "I'll lose the offer" — it's "I'll never find out what they would have paid." The whole point of the reframe is that this is *collaborative*: you and the recruiter are jointly searching for the overlap between what you'll happily sign and what their comp team will approve. Done warmly, negotiating doesn't endanger the relationship — it starts it on a footing of mutual respect.

> [!NOTE]
> **Setup — Candidate Situation & The Hidden Playbook**
>
> **Candidate:** "Priya," senior backend engineer, ~8 years' experience, Java/Spring on payments infrastructure. She has just cleared the loop at **"NorthCo,"** a large product company, for a Senior Software Engineer role. She believes the role is scoped at the *top* of NorthCo's senior band and possibly bordering the next level up.
>
> **Her alternative (BATNA):** a genuine, written competing offer from **"WestStack"** — total compensation around **₹78L/year equivalent** (base + equity + bonus), which she is willing to take if NorthCo doesn't move. She also currently has a stable, well-paid job she is not desperate to leave. A real BATNA is the whole game: she can walk, and she knows it.
>
> **Her target:** a meaningful total-comp uplift over both her current pay and the WestStack number, ideally landing around **₹92–98L/year total**, and — if the data supports it — a conversation about whether she's actually being slotted a level too low.
>
> **Her hidden playbook (memorize this list — every move below executes one line of it):**
> - **Anchor with a researched range, and let them name the first number.** Whoever speaks a number first frames the conversation; make the company go first, then anchor *up* from there with justification.
> - **Never reveal current salary, and never volunteer a target before you have to.** Your current pay is irrelevant to your future value and only drags the offer down. Deflect every probe for it.
> - **Negotiate total compensation, not just base.** Base, equity (vesting over 4 years), and sign-on are three separate levers; trade across all of them.
> - **Justify with scope and market data, not need.** "I'm worth X because the role is scoped at Y and the market pays Z," never "I need X because rent."
> - **Get every revised number in writing before you accept anything.** Verbal offers are not offers.
> - **Stay warm. Be collaborative, never combative.** The recruiter is the person who argues your case to the comp committee — keep them rooting for you.
> - **Tell the truth.** Use the leverage you actually have; never invent a competing offer or inflate a number. A bluff that gets called ends the deal and your reputation.

## The Transcript

### Phase 1 — The Verbal Offer And The Probe

**Recruiter (Marcus):** Priya, great news — the team loved you, and I'm thrilled to be calling with an offer. Everyone on the panel was impressed, especially with the payments depth. We'd love to bring you on as a Senior Software Engineer. Before I walk you through the package — I want to make sure we land this in the right place — **what are your compensation expectations? And do you mind sharing what you're making currently so I can benchmark?**

**Candidate:** Marcus, thank you — that genuinely means a lot, and I'm excited too; the team is exactly the kind of problem space I want to be in. On the numbers — I'd actually love to hear the package you've put together first. You all know how the role is scoped internally better than I do, so it makes sense for me to react to your proposal rather than guess at it. What did the team land on?

> [!TIP]
> Textbook opening. Marcus asked two things at once — your *expectations* and your *current salary* — and Priya answered neither while sounding delighted to be talking. The move is "you go first." The party who names a number first sets the anchor; by bouncing it back warmly ("you know the scope better than I do"), she keeps the framing power without a hint of friction. Note she did **not** say "I'd rather not share" — that sounds defensive. She redirected to *their* number, which is a perfectly natural thing to want to hear first.

**Recruiter:** Ha, fair enough. Okay — here's where we are. We're offering a **base of ₹38L**, an **equity grant of ₹40L vesting over four years** — so ₹10L a year — and a **₹6L signing bonus** in year one. That puts your year-one total right around **₹54L**, and steady-state around ₹48L. It's a strong offer for the senior band — we're genuinely excited.

**Candidate:** That's a thoughtful package, and I appreciate you laying it out cleanly — base, equity, and sign-on, that's exactly how I think about it too. Let me take it in. And honestly, to your earlier question, my current base is three—

> [!WARNING]
> **STOP — the near-miss.** Priya is two syllables from saying her current salary out loud. This is the single most expensive mistake in the entire negotiation. If her current pay is *below* this offer, she has just told Marcus the offer already delights her and he never needs to move. If it's *above*, she's invited him to "match" her current number instead of paying for her future value — capping the upside. Either way, her current salary is **irrelevant** to what this role is worth, and disclosing it hands the anchor to the other side. The reflex to "be helpful and honest" is exactly what the probe is engineered to exploit. Watch her catch it.

**Candidate:** — actually, let me restart that thought, because my current pay isn't really the right yardstick here. What I'm anchored to is the *market* for this scope and what the role is worth to NorthCo. So rather than benchmark backwards off my current package, I'd rather talk about where this offer sits against the market for the work. Can I share where my head is, with the homework behind it?

**Recruiter:** Sure, go ahead.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The recovery, and why it works.** Priya didn't freeze or get cagey — she narrated the pivot ("that's not the right yardstick") so the redirect sounded like *reasoning*, not evasion. Crucially, she never lied. She didn't say "I'm not allowed to share" or invent a number; she made the honest, true case that current pay is the wrong benchmark for future value. Deflection is not deception. In several jurisdictions it is now illegal for an employer to *require* your salary history — but even where it's legal to ask, you are never obligated to answer, and a warm redirect costs you nothing.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> **Meta-insight — leverage is your BATNA, not your bravado.** Everything Priya does from here works *only* because she has a real alternative. Her WestStack offer and her willingness to stay put mean the worst case of this call is "she keeps a great job or takes a great offer." That is what lets her speak calmly and ask for more — she is negotiating from genuine indifference to any single outcome, which is the most powerful position in any negotiation. Candidates with no BATNA often *feel* they have to take the first number, and that fear leaks into the call. The lesson isn't "act confident"; it's *build the alternative before you negotiate* — run your interviews in batches so offers land in the same window, and the confidence is simply true. Never fabricate a competing offer to manufacture leverage: it's unethical, recruiters compare notes, and a bluff that gets called collapses the deal.

### Phase 2 — Anchoring With A Researched Range

Before reading Priya's anchor, see *how she built it*. An anchor that lands is never a wish or a gut number — it is a researched range, decomposed into the same levers the company uses, with a one-line justification for each. Here is the homework she did the week before the call, the kind of thing you should have on a single sheet of paper in front of you.

> [!NOTE]
> **Worked Example — Building The Anchor From The Data.** Priya's process, step by step:
>
> **Step 1 — Pin the level and the market.** She is interviewing for *Senior* backend, payments depth, in her metro. On a levels.fyi-style aggregator she filters to that title, that region, and companies of NorthCo's size and stage, and reads the **band**, not a single point: roughly the 50th-to-90th-percentile total-comp range for that level. She cross-checks with a second source (Pave / a salary survey / two trusted friends at peer companies) so she isn't anchoring off one noisy data point.
>
> **Step 2 — Decompose total comp into the four real components.** "Salary" is a trap; offers are built from four separate buckets, and you must compare *total*, normalized per year:
>
> | Component | What it is | How to annualize |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Base** | Cash salary, paid monthly | Already per-year |
> | **Equity** | Stock/RSU grant, vesting over (usually) 4 years | Grant ÷ 4 = per-year value |
> | **Sign-on bonus** | One-time cash, year one only | Counts in year-one total, *not* steady-state |
> | **Annual/target bonus** | Performance bonus, % of base | base × target % |
>
> **Step 3 — Read the band as numbers.** Her sources put *top-of-senior* total comp at roughly **₹90L–₹1Cr/year**, decomposing typically as **base high-₹40s to low-₹50s**, **equity ₹56L–₹68L over four years** (so ₹14L–₹17L/year), plus **sign-on and bonus** on top. The *midpoint* of the band is ~₹85L; the *top*, where she believes the scope sits, is ~₹95L–₹1Cr.
>
> **Step 4 — Place the scope on the band.** This is the judgment step. The loop described *end-to-end ownership of the ledger reconciliation service and on-call for money movement* — that is top-of-band responsibility, not median. So she anchors at the **top of the researched range, not the middle**, and she can say *why* in one sentence.
>
> **Step 5 — Pick the anchor: slightly above the true target, decomposed across levers.** Her honest target is **₹92–98L**. So she anchors a touch above it, at **~₹95L+**, and — critically — she splits it into all three negotiable levers so there are multiple dials to turn:
>
> | Lever | Anchor | One-line justification |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Base** | ₹52L | High-₹40s-to-low-₹50s is the band's top; scope is top-of-band. |
> | **Equity (4 yrs)** | ₹64L (₹16L/yr) | Mid-to-upper of the ₹56L–₹68L market band for this level. |
> | **Sign-on** | ₹12L | Bridges year one to the steady-state equity ramp; standard ask. |
> | **→ Year-one total** | **~₹95L** | Top-of-band total for the scope described. |
>
> **Why slightly-above-target, not at-target:** negotiation moves you *down* from your anchor, so anchoring at your true minimum guarantees you end up below it. Anchoring modestly above pulls the negotiated midpoint up to where you actually want to be. Note "modestly" and "defensibly" — the anchor is still *inside the real market band she can cite*. A number plucked from fantasy ("₹1.5Cr!") isn't an anchor; it's a credibility leak that makes the recruiter discount everything else you say.

**Candidate:** So here's my thinking, and I want to be transparent about the basis for it. For a senior backend engineer with payments-infrastructure depth, the levels.fyi and Pave data I've been looking at for this market puts total comp for the *top* of the senior band in the **₹90L to ₹1Cr range**, with base in the high ₹40s to low ₹50s. The scope you described in the loop — owning the ledger reconciliation service end to end, on-call for money-movement — reads to me like the top of that band, not the middle. Concretely, I'd be looking for something closer to a **₹52L base, ₹64L in equity over four years, and a ₹12L sign-on** — call it around **₹95L year one**. I'm also weighing a competing offer, which I'll come to, but I want you to hear the market case first.

**Recruiter:** That's a big jump from where we started — base alone is ₹14L over our number.

**Candidate:** It is a real gap, and I'm not expecting you to close all of it in one move — I'd rather we work toward a number we're both happy to sign. The reason I'm anchored where I am is the *scope*, not a wish: end-to-end ownership of money movement is a level of responsibility that the market prices at the top of the band. I've put the comparables together — happy to share the breakdown so we're working off the same data, not competing anecdotes.

> [!TIP]
> Notice the anchor is **a range backed by sources and tied to scope**, and it is *above* her actual target — she wants ~₹92–98L and anchored at ₹95L+ with a credible path to it. Anchoring high (but defensibly) drags the negotiated midpoint up; if she'd opened at her true minimum, she'd have negotiated *down* from there. She also broke the ask into all three levers (base / equity / sign-on) so there are multiple dials to turn. And the justification is **scope and market**, never need — "the work is priced at the top of the band," not "I'd like more money." Offering to share the data turns it from a demand into a joint fact-finding exercise.

**Recruiter:** And the competing offer — can you tell me about it?

**Candidate:** Yes. I have a written offer from another company at around **₹78L total** — strong base, smaller equity. I'm being straight with you: NorthCo is my first choice; the team and the problem space here are a better fit, and I'd rather be here. But ₹78L is my floor in the market right now, and the package as it stands is below it on equity and sign-on. If we can close the gap toward the market number, this is an easy yes for me — today, even.

**Recruiter:** That's helpful, and I appreciate you being upfront that we're your first choice. Would you be able to forward the competing offer, or the comp portion of it?

**Candidate:** I'm comfortable confirming the total figure and that it's in writing, and I'm happy to talk it through — I'd rather keep the other company's document private out of professional courtesy, the same way I'd keep yours private. But you have my word it's real, and ₹78L is the number.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Using a competing offer without weaponizing it.** Three things make this clean. First, it is *true* — she has the document. Second, she frames NorthCo as her **first choice**, which removes the implicit threat and tells Marcus that money is the only thing between him and a yes — exactly what he wants to hear, because now closing the gap *closes the deal*. Third, she declines to forward the rival's paperwork on a principle (courtesy / confidentiality) that also happens to protect her, and she offers her *word* plus the headline number instead. You are generally not obligated to surrender another company's offer letter; confirming the number is plenty. Never, ever invent or inflate the figure — it is unethical and recruiters in the same market talk.

> [!WARNING]
> **Don't turn a competing offer into a hostage situation.** The failure mode here is "match this in 24 hours or I walk." Ultimatums make the recruiter defensive and can poison the relationship you'll rely on for years if you join. Priya does the opposite: warm, first-choice, "easy yes if we close the gap." Leverage works best when it's stated once, calmly, and then you let the other side problem-solve toward you.

### Phase 3 — Trading Base vs Equity vs Sign-On, And The Level Question

**Recruiter:** Let me be honest about our constraints, because I'd rather problem-solve with you than play games. Base is the hardest lever for me — it's tied to the level band and to internal equity with the rest of the team, so I have very little room there; ₹38L is near the top of what I can do for the senior band without an exception. Equity and sign-on I have more flexibility on. If base is roughly fixed, where would you want me to put the money?

**Candidate:** I really appreciate the transparency — that actually helps me prioritize. If base is constrained by the band, then two things. First, on the money itself: I'd weight it toward **equity**, because I'm betting on NorthCo long-term, with a meaningful **sign-on** to bridge year one. Something like base at ₹40L if you can stretch even a little, equity up to ₹60L over four years, and a ₹12L sign-on would get us very close. But second — and I want to raise this carefully — you just said ₹38L is near the *ceiling* of the senior band, and the scope we discussed is end-to-end ownership of money movement. That makes me want to ask directly: **is this role actually being leveled correctly?** If the scope is genuinely senior-plus, the cleaner fix might be the level, not an exception within the band.

**Recruiter:** That's a fair question, and you're not the first to raise it. I can't change the level on this call — that's a conversation with the hiring manager and the bar-raiser. But I can take it back to them. If they agree the scope is next-level, the whole band moves and your base concern solves itself. Let me float two paths internally and come back to you: one, top-of-band senior with maxed equity and sign-on; two, a re-level conversation. Fair?

**Candidate:** That's more than fair — and exactly the collaborative approach I was hoping for. Thank you for being willing to take the level question back rather than shutting it down. I'm in no rush; I'd rather you have the real conversation with the team than rush a number. Take the few days you need.

> [!TIP]
> Two senior moves stacked here. **One:** when the recruiter tells you base is constrained by the band, *believe him and use it* — base genuinely is sticky because of internal-equity rules, so Priya redirects her ask to the levers he said are flexible (equity, sign-on). Fighting hardest on the lever with least give wastes leverage. **Two:** she converts "base is at the band ceiling" into the **level** question. If the offer is bumping the top of the band, the honest possibility is that the role is scoped a level higher — and a level bump moves base, equity, *and* future raises all at once. That's a far bigger prize than squeezing ₹2L of base. She raises it as a genuine question ("is this leveled correctly?"), not a demand, which lets the recruiter become her ally in arguing it internally.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Patience is leverage.** "Take the few days you need" sounds passive; it is the opposite. Priya has a stable current job and a written backup, so *time is on her side* — every day she's not desperate is a day the recruiter has to work toward her. Manufacturing urgency ("I need an answer today") is a tell of weakness. Real BATNA means you can afford to wait, and letting the recruiter run a proper internal conversation produces a better, more durable result than a rushed verbal patch.

### Phase 4 — Handling The "This Is Our Best" Pushback

*[A few days later — the second call.]*

**Recruiter:** Priya, thanks for your patience. I went to bat for you. Two things. On level — the team looked hard at it, and for now they're holding it at senior; the bar-raiser felt the next level needs a track record of org-wide impact you'd build *here*, and they've committed to an accelerated review at your first cycle. On comp, I pushed and got an exception: **base ₹40L, equity ₹56L over four years, sign-on ₹12L** — that's about **₹86L year one, ₹54L steady-state.** I'll be straight with you — **this is genuinely the best I can do; it's our final number.**

**Candidate:** Marcus, thank you — I can tell you actually fought for this, the movement on base and the exception say so, and the accelerated-review commitment matters to me. Let me be equally straight back. This is a real, strong improvement and I'm close. The remaining gap is the equity: I was anchored to ₹64L over four years, and we're at ₹56L. If we can find ₹8L more in the grant — bringing year-one total to around ₹92L — I'll sign today and stop my other conversation. Is there any room left specifically in the equity, given base and sign-on are set?

**Recruiter:** "This is our best" — when I say that, I usually mean it. But let me hear it: ₹8L more in equity and you sign today, done, no further asks?

**Candidate:** Yes — that's the deal. ₹8L more on the equity grant, the rest exactly as you've structured it, and I accept on this call and withdraw from the other process. I want to join; I just want us to land at a number that reflects the scope, and ₹92L does that for me.

> [!TIP]
> **"This is our best" is a position, not a fact — test it without calling the recruiter a liar.** Priya doesn't argue *whether* it's final; she narrows the remaining gap to **one specific, smaller, easy-to-grant lever** (₹8L of equity, not base, not the level) and attaches a **clear close**: "find this and I sign today and stop interviewing." That trade — certainty and a signed candidate in exchange for a small, flexible-bucket concession — is exactly what unlocks a "final" number, because it gives the recruiter something concrete to take back. Note she also did *not* re-litigate the level after a real review declined it; she banked the accelerated-review commitment, conceded the point gracefully, and spent her remaining ask on the one thing still moveable.

> [!WARNING]
> **Don't fold the instant you hear "final," and don't nibble forever either.** Two opposite failure modes. Folding: many candidates hear "best I can do" and accept immediately — leaving real money on the table, because "best" frequently has one more increment in the flexible buckets. Nibbling: asking for "just a little more" five times *after* they've moved burns the goodwill you'll need on day one and can make a recruiter pull an exception. Priya threads it — *one* more specific ask, bounded by an explicit "and then I sign." When you say "I'll sign today if X," you must actually sign if they grant X. Your word is the currency.

**Recruiter:** Okay. Let me make one call — I think I can get the ₹8L from the equity pool since base is locked. If I can, we're done at ₹92L year one and I send paper today. Give me twenty minutes.

### Phase 5 — Closing And Getting It In Writing

**Recruiter:** Done. I got it. Final package: **base ₹40L, equity ₹64L vesting over four years — 25% a year, one-year cliff — sign-on ₹12L paid in your first month, ₹92L year-one total.** Plus the accelerated promo review at your first cycle. Congratulations, Priya — I'm really glad we got here.

**Candidate:** That's fantastic — I'm genuinely happy, and thank you for going back three times. I'm accepting. One thing before we hang up, just so we're perfectly aligned: could you put the full package in the **written offer letter** — base, the equity grant amount *and* the vesting schedule and cliff, the sign-on amount and its payment timing, and the accelerated-review commitment in writing too? Once I see all of that reflected in the letter, I'll sign the same day. Is the accelerated review something that can go in the letter, or is it tracked separately?

**Recruiter:** Base, equity, vesting, and sign-on all go in the formal letter — that's standard. The accelerated-review commitment usually lives in a separate note from the hiring manager rather than the offer letter itself, but I'll get it to you in an email from him so it's documented. You'll have everything today.

**Candidate:** Perfect — an email from the hiring manager works for the review commitment, as long as it's written down somewhere I can point to later. I'll watch for the letter and the note, review them, and sign today. Marcus, thank you — this was a genuinely good process, and I'm excited to start.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Get. It. In. Writing.** A verbal "₹92L and an accelerated review" is worth nothing if it isn't in a document. Priya does the closing checklist precisely: she names *every component* that must appear in the letter — base, **grant amount and the vesting schedule and cliff** (the same headline equity number means very different things over 3 vs 4 years, or with a backloaded vest), sign-on **and its payment timing/clawback**, and the review commitment. When she learns the promo commitment lives outside the formal letter, she doesn't shrug — she pins it to a *written email from the hiring manager* so there's a paper trail. "I'll sign once I see it in writing" is the correct final posture: enthusiastic, committed, and contingent on documentation.

> [!TIP]
> **End warm, and stop.** Once she has the yes she wants, Priya *closes* — she accepts, thanks him genuinely, and doesn't squeeze for one more thing. The relationship with this recruiter and hiring manager outlives the negotiation; you'll be working with these people. A clean, gracious close ("this was a good process, I'm excited to start") is the difference between joining with goodwill and joining with a recruiter who feels nickel-and-dimed. Get the documentation, sign promptly, and start the job with the relationship intact.

## The Phrasebook — Weak Line vs Strong Line

The transcript shows the *moves*; this is the *words*. For each pressure point, here is the line that quietly costs you money (usually because it overshares or sounds defensive) next to the line that holds the framing. Say the strong versions out loud until they're reflexes — under the adrenaline of a real offer call, you'll reach for whatever you've rehearsed.

### The "What's Your Current Comp / Expectation" Probe

> [!WARNING]
> **Weak / oversharing:** "Sure! I'm currently making ₹34L base, and I'm hoping to get to around ₹45L." → You just handed over the anchor *and* capped your own ceiling ₹50L below market. Game over before it started.
>
> **Weak / defensive:** "I'd rather not disclose that." → True, but it sounds guarded and creates friction in the first thirty seconds.

> [!TIP]
> **Strong / anchored:** "Thank you — I'm genuinely excited about this team. On numbers, I'd love to hear the package you've put together first; you know how the role is scoped internally better than I do, so it makes sense for me to react to your proposal. What did the team land on?" → Warm, no number, framing intact, bounces the first-move back to them.
>
> **If pushed a second time** ("I really do need a ballpark to take to the team"): "Totally understand — I'm calibrating to the market for this scope rather than my current package, which isn't really the right benchmark for the work here. Happy to react to a concrete number from you, and I'll be straight with you about whether it's in range." → Still no number, still warm, reframes to *scope and market* without lying.

### Anchoring (After They Name Their Number)

> [!WARNING]
> **Weak:** "That's a bit low. Could you do a little better? Maybe ₹45L?" → No basis, no levers, and you've quietly revealed ₹45L is your real target — so now you negotiate *down* from there.

> [!TIP]
> **Strong:** "That's a thoughtful package — base, equity, sign-on is exactly how I think about it. Based on levels.fyi and Pave for senior backend with payments depth, top-of-band total comp is in the ₹90L–₹1Cr range. The scope you described — end-to-end ownership of money movement — reads to me like the top of that band. So I'd be looking at roughly base ₹52L, equity ₹64L over four years, and a ₹12L sign-on, around ₹95L year one. Happy to share the comparables so we're on the same data." → Range, sources, scope justification, all three levers, slightly above target, offered as joint fact-finding.

### The "This Is Our Best / Final Number" Pushback

> [!WARNING]
> **Weak / fold:** "Oh, okay — that works, I'll take it." → "Best" almost always has one more increment in the flexible buckets; you'll never know.
>
> **Weak / combative:** "Come on, that can't really be the most you can do." → Calls the recruiter a liar; makes them defensive; can make them *pull* an exception.

> [!TIP]
> **Strong:** "I can tell you actually fought for this, and the movement on base plus the accelerated-review commitment matter to me. I'm close. The one remaining gap is the equity — I was anchored to ₹64L and we're at ₹56L. If we can find ₹8L more in the grant, bringing year-one total to ~₹92L, I'll sign today and stop my other conversation. Is there any room left specifically in equity, given base and sign-on are set?" → Doesn't dispute "final"; narrows to *one small, specific, flexible-bucket* ask; attaches an explicit close.

### Getting It In Writing

> [!WARNING]
> **Weak:** "Great, I accept! Send the paperwork whenever." → A verbal "₹92L and an accelerated review" with no document is worth nothing; the review promise especially tends to evaporate.

> [!TIP]
> **Strong:** "That's fantastic — I'm accepting. One thing so we're perfectly aligned: could the written letter reflect the full package — base, the equity grant amount *and* the vesting schedule and cliff, the sign-on amount and its payment timing, and the accelerated-review commitment in writing too? Once I see all of that, I'll sign the same day. Is the review something that can go in the letter, or is it tracked separately?" → Itemizes every component, pins the soft promise to a paper trail, stays enthusiastic and contingent on documentation.

## Debrief & Scorecard

Assessing **negotiation effectiveness** — not interview performance. The technical loop was already passed; this is purely about how well she converted a strong position into a strong package.

| Dimension | Did Well / Missed | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Let them anchor first** | Did well | Bounced the "what are your expectations?" probe back warmly; made NorthCo name ₹54L first, then anchored above it. |
| **Protecting current salary** | Did well (after a near-miss) | Almost disclosed it; caught herself and reframed to market/scope. Recovery was honest, not evasive — the ideal handling. |
| **Anchoring high + defensibly** | Did well | Opened at ~₹95L+ with cited comparables and scope justification, above her true ₹92–98L target — pulled the midpoint up. |
| **Negotiating total comp, not base** | Did well | Treated base / equity / sign-on as three levers; when told base was band-locked, redirected the ask to equity and sign-on. |
| **Using the competing offer** | Did well | True figure, framed NorthCo as first choice, declined to forward the rival's letter on principle, never inflated. |
| **Raising the level question** | Did well | Converted "base is at band ceiling" into a leveling conversation — the highest-value lever — and made the recruiter an ally on it. |
| **Handling "this is final"** | Did well | Tested it with one specific, small, flexible-bucket ask (+₹8L equity) bound to an explicit "and I sign today." |

**What got left on the table (the honest critique):**
- **She accepted the level hold somewhat quickly.** A real review declined it, so conceding was correct — but she could have asked for the accelerated-review commitment to be *more concrete*: a defined timeline ("reviewed at the 6-month mark, not just 'first cycle'") and the explicit criteria for the bump, in writing. "Accelerated review" with no date is a soft promise.
- **She didn't probe the equity *refresh* and vesting cliff economics.** Year-one total is ₹92L, but steady-state drops to ~₹54L once the sign-on lapses and before any refresh grant. A sharper close asks: what's the typical annual refresh, and what's the cliff exposure if I leave in year one? The headline number can hide a year-four cliff.
- **She left base where the band capped it** — correct given internal-equity constraints, but she could have asked one clarifying question: "what would it take to get a band exception on base specifically?" Sometimes the answer reveals more room than "it's locked" implied.

**What she did right (the takeaways):**
- Had a **real BATNA** and let it do the work calmly — never fabricated, never threatened.
- **Made the company go first**, then anchored high with *scope and market data*, never need.
- Treated it as a **joint problem** ("let's land where we're both happy"), which kept the recruiter on her side and unlocked the level conversation and the final exception.
- **Closed once she got her number, and got everything documented** — enthusiasm plus a paper trail.

## Variations

The same playbook flexes to weaker and stronger positions — the principles don't change, only the leverage does.

- **No competing offer.** You lose the BATNA lever, so lean harder on *market data and scope* and on your *current job's stability* ("I'm happy where I am; it would take a compelling package to move"). Anchor on comparables, not a rival number. Never *invent* a competing offer to fill the gap — it's unethical and recruiters cross-check. With no BATNA, your fallback leverage is genuine willingness to stay put; if you don't even have that, negotiate gently on the flexible buckets (sign-on, equity) where a "no" costs you nothing.
- **Exploding offer ("you have 48 hours").** A real deadline that pressures you before your other processes finish is a leverage play. Respond calmly: ask for a specific extension with a reason ("I have a final round Thursday; can we talk Friday?"), and gauge the reaction — a company that *cannot* give you a few days is signaling how it'll treat you later. If they truly won't move and you have a strong BATNA, an exploding offer is information about the employer. Never let an artificial clock stampede you into skipping the written-confirmation step.
- **Internal promotion / counter-offer.** Negotiating *up* a level inside your current company runs on the same scope-and-market logic, but two differences: your manager is your *advocate*, not an adversary, so frame it as "help me make the case," and your leverage is partly your demonstrated track record plus, sometimes, an external offer. Be cautious accepting a *retention counter* to a resignation — the reasons you wanted to leave usually remain, and accepting can mark you as a flight risk. Negotiate the promotion on its merits, ideally *before* you're holding an external offer.

Those three deserve to be *played out*, because the words change even when the playbook doesn't. Here is each scenario as a short scene, with the lines that work.

### Scenario A — The Exploding Offer ("You Have 48 Hours")

You have NorthCo's offer in hand, but you also have a final round scheduled at another company on Thursday. Then the recruiter adds time pressure.

**Recruiter:** We'd love to have you, but I'll be honest — this offer is only good through end of day tomorrow. We have other strong candidates and I need to know.

> [!WARNING]
> **Weak / stampeded:** "Okay, okay — let me just say yes now so I don't lose it." → You've let an artificial clock skip your other process and your written-confirmation step. A deadline that exists to stop you from finishing your alternatives is a *tactic*, not a constraint.

> [!TIP]
> **Strong / calm with a specific reason:** "I'm genuinely excited and NorthCo is a real contender for me. I want to give you a committed yes, not a panicked one. I have a final-round commitment Thursday that I can't ethically cancel; could we talk Friday? That's two business days. If the concern is that I'm not serious, I'm happy to tell you NorthCo is at the top of my list." → Asks for a *specific, short* extension with a *reason*, and reassures on intent rather than caving.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **An exploding offer is information about the employer.** Watch the reaction. A reasonable company gives you a few days — hiring is a months-long process; nobody sane loses a great candidate over 48 hours versus 96. If they *genuinely cannot* extend a two-day request, you've learned how they'll treat you when you need flexibility *after* you join. With a strong BATNA, you can call the bluff: "I understand if the timing doesn't work on your end; I'd hate to rush a decision this big and get it wrong for both of us." Often the deadline softens the moment it stops working. Never let the clock push you past the written-confirmation step.

### Scenario B — No Competing Offer (Leverage From Being A Strong Candidate)

Same offer, but you have *no* rival in hand. You lost the BATNA lever — so you lean on market data, scope, and the fact that they already chose you.

**Recruiter:** And do you have any other offers we should know about?

> [!WARNING]
> **Weak / fabricate:** "Yeah, I've got another offer at ₹90L." (you don't) → Unethical, and recruiters in the same market cross-check; if it's called, the deal and your reputation are gone. Never invent leverage.
>
> **Weak / collapse:** "No, nothing else — so honestly whatever you can offer is fine." → You've announced you have no alternative *and* pre-folded. The offer will sit exactly where it started.

> [!TIP]
> **Strong / honest, market-anchored:** "I'm not running a bidding war — I'm focused on NorthCo specifically. I'll be straight: I'm happy in my current role, so it would take a package that reflects the market for this scope to move me. From levels.fyi and Pave, top-of-senior for this work is ₹90L–₹1Cr, and the scope you described sits at the top of that band. The current number is below that on equity and sign-on. If we can close that gap, this is a yes." → No fabricated rival; the leverage is *genuine willingness to stay put* plus *cited market data* plus *the simple fact that they invested weeks to choose you and don't want to restart the search.*

> [!INTERVIEW]
> **You have more leverage than "no offer" feels like.** The company has already paid the cost of finding you — re-opening the req means weeks of lost engineer-time, a slipping roadmap, and a hiring manager who has to start over. "We picked you" is itself leverage. If you don't even have the willingness-to-stay fallback (you need *this* job), don't bluff — negotiate *gently* on the buckets where a "no" costs nothing: a larger sign-on, a slightly bigger equity grant, an earlier review. The downside of a polite, well-reasoned ask on the flexible buckets is essentially zero.

### Scenario C — The Internal Promotion Negotiation

You're not changing companies — you want a level bump *inside* your current org. Your manager is your ally, not your counterparty.

**You (to your manager):** I'd like to make the case that my scope has grown into the next level, and I'd love your help building it. Over the last year I've owned the ledger reconciliation service end to end, led the on-call rotation for money movement, and mentored two engineers through their ramp. That's senior-plus scope by our own rubric. Can we look at the leveling criteria together and figure out what, if anything, is still missing?

> [!TIP]
> **Frame it as "help me make the case," not a demand.** Your manager argues for you in the calibration room you're not in — so arm them. Bring *evidence mapped to the leveling rubric* (scope owned, impact shipped, people grown), not a salary ask. The comp follows the level; win the level and the money comes with it. "Help me close the gap" makes your manager a collaborator; "I deserve a raise" makes them defensive.

> [!WARNING]
> **Be very careful with the retention counter-offer.** If you resign and your company suddenly offers more to keep you, remember: the reasons you wanted to leave (scope, manager, growth, the comp you had to *threaten to quit* to get) usually remain — and accepting can quietly mark you as a flight risk in the next planning cycle. Statistically, a large share of people who accept a counter-offer have left within a year anyway. **Negotiate the promotion on its merits and on its own timeline — ideally well *before* you're holding an external offer**, so it's a story about your growth, not a hostage exchange. If you *do* use an external offer as a forcing function, know you may be spending trust you can't get back.

## Practice

Build your own negotiation muscle before you're live on the call:

1. **Script your deflection.** Write the exact sentence you'll say when asked "what's your current salary?" and "what are your expectations?" Practice it out loud until it sounds warm and natural, not defensive. ("I'd love to hear the package you've put together first — you know the scope better than I do.")
2. **Build three anchors.** For a target role and level, find real comparables (levels.fyi, Pave, salary surveys) and write a base / equity / sign-on anchor that's defensibly *above* your true target, with the scope justification for each. Say the whole ask aloud in one breath.
3. **Rehearse the "this is final" response.** Draft the one specific, small, flexible-bucket ask you'd make, bound to a clear "and I'll sign today." Then draft how you'd *gracefully concede* if the answer is a genuine no.
4. **Write your closing checklist.** List every component that must appear in writing — base, equity *amount + vesting schedule + cliff*, sign-on *amount + timing + clawback*, and any verbal commitments — and the exact sentence you'll use to request it all in the offer letter.
5. **Stress-test your BATNA.** Honestly assess your alternative. If it's weak, the highest-leverage move isn't a better script — it's *generating a real alternative* by batching your interviews so offers land in the same window.
6. **Do the anchor math on a real role.** Pick a target title + level + region. Open levels.fyi (or Pave / a survey), read the *band* not a point, and fill in the four-bucket table: base, equity-over-4-years, sign-on, target bonus. Write down the band's midpoint and top as numbers, decide where your scope sits, and pick an anchor *slightly above your true target but still inside the cite-able band*. One sentence of justification per lever. This sheet is what you put in front of you on the call.
7. **Run the weak-vs-strong drill.** For each phrasebook moment — the comp probe, the anchor, the "this is final" pushback, getting it in writing — say the *weak* line out loud, notice how it leaks money or sounds defensive, then say the *strong* line until it's the one that comes out under pressure.
8. **Rehearse all three scenario variations.** Role-play the exploding offer (ask for a specific short extension with a reason), the no-competing-offer case (market data + willingness to stay, never a fabricated rival), and the internal promotion ("help me make the case," mapped to the leveling rubric). Have a friend play the recruiter and push back.

## Recap

- **It's collaborative, not adversarial.** The recruiter's incentive is to get you to yes; the goal is a number you'll happily sign and their comp team will approve. Stay warm — they argue your case internally.
- **Let them name the first number; never volunteer your current salary or your target early.** Whoever anchors first frames the deal. Deflect the salary probe warmly and honestly — current pay is the wrong yardstick for future value.
- **Anchor high but defensibly, on scope and market data, not need.** Above your true target, with cited comparables.
- **Negotiate total comp across all three levers** — base, equity (watch vesting/cliff), sign-on — and redirect to the flexible buckets when base is band-locked. Convert a band-ceiling base into a *level* conversation when the scope justifies it.
- **Use a real BATNA calmly; never fabricate one.** Frame the company as your first choice, state the number once, let them problem-solve toward you. Patience is leverage.
- **"This is final" is a position — test it with one small, specific ask bound to an immediate yes — then stop.** Don't fold instantly; don't nibble forever.
- **Get every number, schedule, and commitment in writing before you sign**, and close warm — you'll be working with these people.
- **This thirty-minute conversation can be worth a large lifetime sum.** The delta compounds through every future raise, refresh, and offer. The cost of asking politely is essentially zero — companies don't rescind for professional negotiation — so the only real risk is never finding out what they'd have paid.
- **Build your anchor from a researched band, decomposed into four buckets** — base, equity-over-4-years, sign-on, target bonus — placed on the band by *scope*, and set *slightly above your true target but inside the cite-able range*. Anchor at your minimum and you negotiate below it.
- **Rehearse the strong lines, not just the strategy.** For each pressure point (comp probe, anchor, "this is final," in-writing) there's a weak line that leaks money or sounds defensive and a strong line that holds the framing — say the strong one until it's a reflex.
- **The playbook flexes to your leverage.** Exploding offer: ask for a specific short extension with a reason, and read the reaction as information. No competing offer: lean on market data and genuine willingness to stay — never fabricate a rival. Internal promotion: your manager is your advocate, so "help me make the case" against the leveling rubric, and treat retention counter-offers with caution.
